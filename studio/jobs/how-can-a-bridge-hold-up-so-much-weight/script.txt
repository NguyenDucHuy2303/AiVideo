How Can a Bridge Hold Up So Much Weight?
Mai (surprised): "Huy, look at that truck! It is heavier than an elephant. Why doesn't the bridge just snap?" Huy (happily): "Great question, Mai. And here is a surprise. In Greece there is a stone bridge about 3,300 years old, and people still use it today."
Huy: "Today we will find out how a bridge can hold up so much weight. We will meet squeezing, pulling, a clever curve, and a shape that refuses to squash."
Mai (proudly): "Easy! Bridges are made of super strong stuff. Thick steel and giant rocks!"
Huy (happily): "That is a smart guess, Mai. Strong materials really do matter, and most big bridges today are made mostly of steel." Huy: "But a pile of rocks is not a bridge. And one stick of dry spaghetti snaps easily, yet students build bridges out of spaghetti that are surprisingly strong. So the secret is not just what a bridge is made of. It is also the shape."
Huy: "Everything on Earth is pulled down by gravity. That pull is what we feel as weight. A bridge has one big job. Catch that weight and pass it along into the strong ground."
Huy: "Let's build the simplest bridge, a beam bridge. A beam is just a long, flat piece, like a plank across a stream. Mai, try this ruler."
Mai (surprised): "When I press the middle, it bends!" Huy (happily): "Yes. The middle is farthest from the supports, so that is where a beam bends the most."
Huy: "Now look closely. When a beam bends, its top gets squeezed together, and its bottom gets stretched apart. One ruler, two jobs at once."
Huy: "Takeaway one. A bridge must pass weight into the ground, and that squeezes or stretches its parts."
Huy: "Engineers, the people who design bridges, have special words for this. Squeezing is called compression. Pulling and stretching is called tension."
Mai (laughing): "Compression, squish! Tension, stretch!" Huy (laughing): "Perfect. You sound like an engineer already."
Huy: "Here is the important part. Stone and brick are very strong when you squeeze them. But pull on them and they crack quite easily. Steel copes well with both."
Mai (worried): "So stone is bad for bridges?" Huy (firmly): "Not at all! Some stone bridges have lasted thousands of years. Builders just had to find a shape that only squeezes the stone and never pulls it."
Huy: "Takeaway two. Every part of a bridge is squeezed or pulled, so builders pick the right material and the right shape for each job."
Huy: "That shape is the arch. When weight presses on the top of an arch, the curve passes it around and down both sides into the ground."
Huy: "An arch is built from wedge shaped stones. A wedge is wide at one end and narrow at the other, like a slice of cake. Each stone presses on its neighbours, so every stone is squeezed. Just what stone likes."
Huy: "The stone at the very top is called the keystone. It locks all the others in place, like the last piece of a puzzle."
Huy: "An arch also pushes outward at its feet, like your feet trying to slide apart when you stand with your legs wide. So builders put strong blocks at each end, called abutments, to push back and hold the arch together."
Mai (surprised): "So if a heavy cart rolls on top, the stones squeeze together even tighter?" Huy (proudly): "Exactly! In old stone arches, more weight made the stones press together harder. Builders even piled rubble, which means broken stones, on top to make the bridge heavier and steadier."
Huy: "That is why arches last. About 2,000 years ago the Romans built hundreds of stone arch bridges, and many are still standing. Some even carry cars today."
Huy: "Takeaway three. An arch turns weight into squeezing and sends it down into strong ground at its ends."
Huy: "Now meet a shape that refuses to squash. Here is a square and a triangle made of sticks, with loose pins at the corners."
Mai (surprised): "The square flopped into a diamond! But the triangle won't budge!" Huy (happily): "A triangle cannot change shape unless one of its sides gets longer or shorter. That makes it super stiff."
Huy: "A bridge made of triangles is called a truss bridge. Each straight piece is mostly just squeezed or just pulled, not bent. And that is exactly what thin pieces handle best."
Huy: "That is the spaghetti secret. One stick snaps when you bend it. But joined in triangles, lots of weak sticks become one strong bridge. Trusses use material well, so they cost less to build too."
Huy: "Engineers mix these ideas. There are five main kinds of bridge. Beam, cantilever, arch, suspension, and cable stayed. A cantilever is an arm that sticks out, held at only one end. Suspension and cable stayed bridges hang the road from steel cables, which are great at being pulled."
Huy: "Before a big bridge is built, engineers research, design and test their ideas, often with models. They make sure it can stand up to heavy loads, strong wind, and even earthquakes."
Huy: "Takeaway four. Triangles keep their shape, so a truss turns many thin pieces into one strong bridge."
Huy: "Here is a check you can try at home. Lay a sheet of paper across two stacks of books, and put a coin in the middle." Mai (sadly): "It flopped right down."
Huy: "Now fold the same paper back and forth, like a paper fan, and lay it across again." Mai (joyfully): "It holds lots of coins! The folds make little triangles!"
Huy: "Or bend a second sheet into a curve under the flat one, with its ends pushed against the books. Now you have an arch, and the books are your abutments." Mai (happily): "Same paper, new shape, much stronger!"
Huy: "So how does a bridge hold so much weight? With clever shapes. Arches, triangles and cables turn heavy weight into squeezing and pulling, and pass it safely into the ground."
Huy: "Fun fact. The Zhaozhou Bridge in China was built of stone about 1,400 years ago, and it still stands. Little extra arches in its sides let floodwater rush through." Mai (surprised): "A bridge with windows!"
Mai (happily): "Huy, if bridges need the ground, how does a giant plane stay up with nothing under it?" Huy (laughing): "Ooh, that is next time's question! Subscribe so you don't miss it. Bye, bridge builders!"
