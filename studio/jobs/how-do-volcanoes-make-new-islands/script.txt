How Do Volcanoes Make New Islands? | Ask Huy
Bối (surprised): "Huy, can the ocean make a brand new island? One that was never there before?" Beo (happily): "A baby island!" Huy (happily): "Yes, new islands really are born! And here is a surprise. The biggest mountain on Earth, measured from its very bottom, is mostly hidden under the sea."
Huy: "Today we find out how volcanoes make new islands, step by step. And we'll see how one hot place deep in the Earth can make a whole line of islands."
Bối: "I think islands float on the water, like giant rafts." Huy (happily): "Good guess! From the beach they do look like they float. But an island is joined to the sea floor, like a tree is joined to the ground. It goes all the way down."
Beo (proudly): "Maybe islands grow from seeds!" Huy (laughing): "Ha! Islands do grow, Beo. But not from seeds. Many of them grow from hot, melted rock."
Huy: "Some low islands are made from sand and coral. But many tall islands, like the islands of Hawaii, were made by volcanoes. Those are the ones we explore today."
Huy: "Deep under our feet, and deep under the sea floor, the Earth is very, very hot. In some places the rock melts. Melted rock under the ground is called magma."
Huy: "When magma comes out of the ground, we give it a new name. We call it lava. A mountain built by lava is called a volcano." Beo (surprised): "Rock soup!" Huy (laughing): "Yes, a bit like rock soup. But far, far too hot to ever go near."
Huy: "The outside of the Earth is cracked into giant pieces, like a cracked eggshell. We call these pieces plates. They move very, very slowly." Hùng (proudly): "I cracked my breakfast egg for science!"
Huy: "Magma often pushes up where two plates meet. It also comes up at special places called hotspots, far from the edges. Scientists think a hotspot is fed by hot rock rising from very deep, maybe about 3,000 kilometres down." Bối (surprised): "Wow, that is so deep!"
Huy (firmly): "Step one takeaway. Magma rises from deep inside the Earth and can break through the sea floor."
Huy: "Now picture the bottom of the deep ocean. It is dark and cold down here. Magma breaks through a crack, and lava comes out under the water."
Huy: "The cold sea water cools the lava fast, and it turns into hard rock. It often makes round lumps that look like pillows. Scientists call it pillow lava." Beo (happily): "A rock pillow! Is it soft?" Bối (laughing): "No, Beo, it's as hard as rock!"
Huy: "Then the volcano erupts again, and again, and again. Each time, new lava spreads over the old rock and hardens. Layer by layer, the mountain grows taller."
Hùng (happily): "Look! Each pancake is one eruption. One, two, three, four." Hùng (worried): "Oops. I ate eruption number four." Bối (laughing): "Hùng, volcanoes don't eat their own layers!"
Huy: "A big underwater volcano that has not reached the surface is called a seamount. Sea plus mount, a mountain in the sea." Huy (firmly): "Step two takeaway. Each eruption adds a layer of rock, and slowly an underwater mountain grows."
Huy: "Most seamounts stay under the water. Their tops are often hundreds or even thousands of metres down. But a few keep on growing."
Huy: "When the top finally pokes above the waves, surprise! A brand new island is born." Bối (joyfully): "Happy birthday, island!"
Huy: "This really happens. In 2009, an underwater volcano called Hunga Tonga, in the Pacific Ocean, grew up above the sea and became an island."
Huy: "So an island is only the tip of a giant mountain. Mauna Kea in Hawaii looks like a mountain on an island. But measured from its bottom on the ocean floor, it is the biggest mountain on Earth!" Beo (surprised): "The island has hidden legs!"
Huy (firmly): "Step three takeaway. A volcanic island is the top of an underwater mountain that grew tall enough to reach the air."
Bối (surprised): "Huy, why are the Hawaiian islands all in a line? Like beads on a string!" Huy (happily): "Great question! The answer is a hotspot."
Huy: "The hotspot stays still, deep down. But the plate above it slides very slowly, like a moving walkway at an airport. A volcano grows over the hotspot. Then the plate carries it away, and a new volcano starts in the same spot."
Huy: "So you get a line. The youngest island sits over the hotspot. The older ones are farther and farther away. A scientist called J. Tuzo Wilson had this idea in 1963."
Huy: "Away from the hotspot, an island stops erupting. Waves slowly wear it down, and over a very long time it sinks. Some end up as flat-topped mountains under the sea." Beo (sadly): "Bye bye, old island." Huy (happily): "Don't be sad, Beo. Over the hotspot, a new one is growing."
Huy: "Right now, near Hawaii, there is an active young volcano still under the sea. Its name is Kamaʻehuakanaloa." Hùng (tired): "Kama, ehu, kana. I need a snack." Huy (laughing): "It used to be called Lōʻihi. Maybe one day, if it grows tall enough, its top will poke out too."
Huy: "Scientists don't all agree about what makes hotspots. Most think a stream of hot rock rises from very deep. Others think the crust gets stretched, and that lets melted rock come up from not so deep. Science is still finding out!"
Huy (firmly): "Step four takeaway. A hotspot that stays still under a moving plate makes a chain of islands, from young to old."
Huy: "Let's try it at home! All you need is a sheet of paper and a felt pen." Hùng (proudly): "The pen is the hotspot. It stays still. Dot, dot, dot!" Hùng (surprised): "Oops. I forgot to move the paper. Now it's one giant blob island!"
Huy: "Now Bối slides the paper slowly, like a plate. Every few seconds, Hùng presses down a dot." Bối (joyfully): "A line of dots! Just like Hawaii!" Beo (happily): "The last dot is the baby island!"
Huy: "Now imagine if you could watch the ocean for millions of years. You'd see islands rise up, ride along on their plate, and slowly sink again, like a very slow parade." Bối (laughing): "I would need a lot of snacks."
Huy: "So how do volcanoes make new islands? Lava erupts on the sea floor, cools into rock, and piles up layer by layer until the top pokes above the waves. And a plate moving over a hotspot can make a whole chain of islands."
Huy (happily): "Fun fact! Scientists have found more than 14,500 seamounts in Earth's oceans. If you put all the known seamounts together, they would cover about as much space as Europe!" Beo (surprised): "That's a lot of hidden mountains!"
Huy (happily): "Next time, we'll go even deeper and ask, what is inside the Earth? Subscribe to Ask Huy so you don't miss it. Bye for now!" Beo (joyfully): "Bye bye, baby island!"
