Why Do Cats Purr? The Secret Inside the Rumble
Mai (surprised): "Huy, listen! My cat Miso is making a tiny motor sound. Is there an engine inside her?" Huy (laughing): "No engine, Mai. That cosy rumble is called a purr."
Huy: "Here is a surprise. Cats do not only purr when they are happy! Today we will find out what a purr is, how a cat makes it, and why cats do it."
Mai (proudly): "That is easy. Cats purr because they are happy!" Huy (happily): "Great guess! Lots of grown-ups think that too, and you are often right. A cat curled up on a warm lap, eating, or resting will often purr."
Huy: "But cats also purr when they feel stressed, or hurt, or unwell. So happy is part of the answer, but not the whole story." Mai (surprised): "Purring when they are hurt? That is strange!" Huy (happily): "It is! Let us solve this mystery, step by step."
Huy: "Step one. What is a purr? A purr is a soft buzzing sound. It sounds a bit like rolling the letter r in your mouth. Rrrrrr." Mai (laughing): "Rrrrrr! I sound just like Miso!"
Huy: "A purr is made of tiny buzzes, very close together. A house cat makes about 20 to 30 of them every second. That is far too fast to count, so they blur together into one long rumble."
Huy: "Here is a clever trick. A cat purrs while it breathes in and also while it breathes out. So the purr can keep going and going without a break." Mai (surprised): "Like a song with no gaps to breathe?" Huy (happily): "Exactly. Some other animals, like foxes, make a purr-like sound only when they breathe out."
Mai (surprised): "I can feel it in my hand! Her whole body is tingling." Huy: "Yes! A purr makes small shakes, called vibrations, that you can feel through the fur."
Huy: "Takeaway one. A purr is a fast buzz, about 20 to 30 times a second, that a cat makes while breathing in and out."
Huy: "Step two. How does a cat make that buzz? Let us peek inside a cat's throat. Here is the voice box. Scientists call it the larynx. You have one too, in your neck."
Huy: "Inside the voice box are two soft flaps called vocal folds. Between them is a little gap, where air goes in and out."
Huy: "Scientists think tiny muscles squeeze this gap shut and let it open again, over and over, very fast. Each time, the moving air gets a little shake. All those shakes make the purr." Mai (happily): "Like flapping a door open and shut, super fast?" Huy (laughing): "A very tiny, very fast door. Yes!"
Huy: "But what tells the muscles when to squeeze? Scientists think it is the brain. A cat's brain has a special rhythm part. It may work like a drummer, keeping a steady beat. Squeeze, open, squeeze, open."
Huy (firmly): "Now, to be honest, scientists are still not completely sure how purring works. Long ago, some people thought the sound came from blood rushing through the chest. Today, many scientists think the brain and voice box work together. Takeaway two. Scientists think the brain and the voice box team up, opening and shutting a tiny gap fast, to make the buzz." Mai (surprised): "So even scientists are still guessing?" Huy (happily): "Yes! Guessing and testing is how science moves forward."
Huy: "Step three. Why do cats purr when they feel good? To find out, let us visit some brand-new kittens. Kittens are born with their eyes closed. They cannot see yet." Mai (worried): "They cannot see? How do they find their mum?" Huy: "Mum stays right beside them, and they snuggle up to drink her milk. After about two weeks, their eyes open."
Huy: "Kittens use their voices very early. They purr most when they are feeding, drinking their mother's milk. While they drink, they push with their front paws, left, right, left, right. This is called kneading, like kneading bread dough."
Mai (laughing): "Miso does that on my blanket! Push, push, push, and she purrs." Huy: "Many grown cats keep this kitten habit. They knead and purr when they feel cosy, often just before a nap."
Huy: "Grown cats also purr when they are resting, eating, or close to someone they like. Look for other happy signs too. A tail held high means friendly. And a slow blink is a bit like a cat smile."
Huy: "Takeaway three. Purring starts when kittens are feeding, and grown cats purr when they feel calm and close to friends."
Huy: "Step four. Now for the mystery. Why would a cat purr when it is stressed, hurt, or feeling unwell?"
Huy: "Think about what you do when you feel nervous. Some people hum a little tune. Some people hug a teddy." Mai (happily): "I hum when there is thunder! It makes me feel braver."
Huy: "Scientists think purring can work a bit like that for cats. A purr may help a cat calm itself down. We call that self-soothing. Purring may even help a cat cope with pain."
Huy: "So a purr does not always mean everything is fine. If a cat is purring but also hiding, or seems unwell, tell the grown-up who looks after it. They will know what to do." Mai: "So I should look at the whole cat, not just listen!" Huy (proudly): "That is exactly what cat experts do."
Huy: "Takeaway four. A purr can mean I feel good. And it can also mean I am helping myself feel better."
Huy: "Imagine if you could purr like a cat. Every time you felt happy, or nervous before a test, your body would rumble. Rrrrrr." Mai (laughing): "Everyone in class would know when I am happy!"
Huy: "Here is a safe thing to try at home. Put your fingers gently on the front of your neck. Now hum a long mmmm. Can you feel the buzz? That is your own voice box shaking, a little like a cat's when it purrs." Mai (surprised): "It tickles my fingers!"
Huy (happily): "One more try. If you know a friendly cat, ask a grown-up first. Then sit quietly, let the cat come to you, look at it, and blink very slowly. Some cats blink slowly back. That is a friendly sign, and it may mean they trust you." Mai (joyfully): "Miso blinked back at me!"
Huy (happily): "So, why do cats purr? A purr is a fast buzz. Scientists think it is made by a cat's brain and voice box working together. Cats purr when they feel happy and safe. They also purr to calm themselves when they feel bad."
Huy: "Here is a fun fact. A lion can roar, but scientists say it cannot purr like a house cat. A cheetah can purr, but it cannot roar. In fact, scientists say no cat can both purr and roar!" Mai (surprised): "So Miso is more like a cheetah than a lion!"
Huy: "Next time, another cat mystery. Do cats really always land on their feet? Subscribe so you do not miss it, and keep asking questions!" Mai (happily): "Bye bye! Rrrrrr!"
