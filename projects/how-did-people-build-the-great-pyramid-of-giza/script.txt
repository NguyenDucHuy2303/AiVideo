How Did People Build the Great Pyramid of Giza?
Bối (surprised): "Huy, this pyramid is gigantic! How did people build it without trucks or cranes?" Beo (surprised): "Maybe a giant built it!" Huy (happily): "Great question, Bối. Here is a surprise. It was built more than 4,500 years ago, and it has about 2,300,000 stone blocks!"
Huy: "Today we find out how the Egyptians cut the stones, moved them, lifted them up, and who did all that hard work. Let's go back in time!" Hùng (laughing): "Time machine ready! Oops, the dial fell off. We'll just pretend."
Bối (firmly): "I know! Slaves built it. Lots of them, forced to work." Beo (proudly): "I think a hundred robots did it!" Huy (happily): "Good guesses, both of you. Robots weren't invented yet, Beo. But Bối, your guess is very sensible. Even the ancient Greeks believed it, long after the pyramid was built."
Huy: "But in 1990, archaeologists found the workers' graves near the pyramid. Archaeologists are scientists who dig up clues from the past. The clues show the builders were workers who got paid. Some worked as a kind of tax, which means paying the king with work instead of money." Bối (surprised): "So they were builders with a real job?" Huy (happily): "Exactly! And how they did it is a story in four steps."
Huy: "The pyramid was built for a king called Khufu. Egyptian kings were called pharaohs. Most of the stone came from right next to the building site. Fine white limestone came from Tura, across the river Nile. Very hard granite came from Aswan, far to the south."
Huy: "A place where people cut stone out of the ground is called a quarry. Workers cut the limestone with copper tools, like chisels, drills and saws." Bối (surprised): "Copper? Isn't copper quite soft?" Huy (happily): "Plain copper is. But theirs had another material mixed in, so it was harder, and hammering it made it harder still."
Bối (worried): "What about the super hard granite?" Huy: "Copper alone can't cut granite. So workers pounded it with an even harder stone, and sawed it with sand under the blade. The tiny sand grains did the scratching. It took a very long time!" Hùng (tired): "I rubbed this pebble with sandpaper for a whole minute. Look, one tiny scratch!"
Huy: "Most blocks weighed about 2.5 tonnes. That's heavier than a family car! A few giant granite blocks were much heavier." Beo (surprised): "Heavier than our car?" Huy (proudly): "Yes, Beo! Step one takeaway. Copper tools, hard stones and sand turned rock into blocks."
Huy: "How do we know how the stones travelled? In 2013, scientists found old writings in a cave in Egypt. They are the diary of an official named Merer, written more than 4,500 years ago." Bối (laughing): "Does it say what he had for lunch?" Huy (laughing): "Sadly not! But it says his team took limestone blocks from Tura to Giza by boat."
Huy: "Boats were the trucks of ancient Egypt, and the river Nile was the big road. A heavy block is much easier to move floating on water than dragged across the desert." Beo (happily): "A boat for rocks!"
Hùng (proudly): "I brought a sled! It's a toy one, though." Huy: "Perfect, Hùng. On land, blocks rode on a sledge. A sledge is a wooden sled, like a snow sled but with no snow. Teams pulled it with ropes. Scientists think water was poured in front to help it slide."
Bối: "How many people did it take to pull one block?" Huy: "One expert worked out that about eight workers could move a block of about 2,750 kilograms on a sledge, if the track was made slippery." Bối (proudly): "Only eight? My football team could do that!" Huy (laughing): "Maybe, after a lot of practice!"
Huy (proudly): "Step two takeaway. Boats carried the stones along the river, and sledges slid them over land."
Huy: "Now for the hardest part. When it was new, the pyramid was about 146 metres tall. That's taller than a football pitch is long, if you stood the pitch on its end!" Bối (worried): "So how do you get a heavy block all the way up there?"
Huy: "Most experts think the answer starts with ramps. A ramp is a sloping path, like a slide you walk up. Builders dragged the sledges up ramps to the higher layers. Remains of small ramps have been found at the Great Pyramid." Beo (joyfully): "A slide! Can I slide down it?" Huy (laughing): "These slides were only for stones, Beo."
Huy: "Ramps were probably helped by levers. A lever is a long, strong pole. Push down on one end, and the other end lifts something heavy a little bit. Lever, nudge, lever again, and the block slowly creeps into place." Hùng (laughing): "Look, I pushed down and the book went up! And then it went down. On my foot."
Bối: "So what did the ramp look like?" Huy: "Here's a secret. Scientists still don't agree! One giant straight ramp would have been enormous, so most experts think it wasn't that. Some think of a zigzag ramp up one side, or a ramp that spirals round the outside."
Huy: "A French architect called Jean-Pierre Houdin has another idea. He thinks a ramp spiralled up inside the pyramid, plus a ramp outside for the big blocks lower down." Bối (surprised): "A ramp hidden inside? That's sneaky!" Huy (happily): "Maybe they used different tricks at different heights. Scientists are still hunting for clues."
Huy (firmly): "Step three takeaway. Ramps and levers got the blocks up, but exactly what the ramps looked like is still a mystery."
Huy: "Before the first block, the ground had to be really flat. Some experts think workers dug long trenches and filled them with water. Still water lies flat, so they could mark a level line." Bối (happily): "Like my bathwater when I stop splashing!" Beo (laughing): "I never stop splashing!"
Huy: "Experts think about 20,000 workers built it, and it took about 20 years. They camped near the pyramid. They were skilled, which means they were very good at their jobs, like cutting, dragging and measuring."
Huy: "The blocks inside weren't perfect. They were roughly cut. Workers filled the gaps with rubble, which means broken bits of stone, and a kind of plaster called gypsum."
Huy: "Last came the outside. Smooth white stones covered the whole pyramid. Most of them are gone now, but you can still see some around the bottom." Bối (joyfully): "So it used to be a giant smooth white triangle!" Beo (surprised): "Like a big snow mountain!"
Huy: "All this needed a planner. Experts think it may have been Hemiunu, the king's top helper." Huy (proudly): "Step four takeaway. Thousands of paid, skilled workers and lots of smart planning built it over about 20 years."
Huy: "Imagine if you had to place 2,300,000 blocks in 20 years. That's about 300 blocks every single day. If you worked ten hours a day, you'd need to place a block about every two minutes!" Hùng (laughing): "I'll try with toy blocks. One, two... oh no, my tower fell!" Bối (tired): "I'm tired just hearing that."
Huy: "Want to test the sledge idea? Hùng will show you." Hùng (happily): "In a sandbox, with a grown-up, put some pebbles in a small box and tie on a string. Pull it over dry sand. Feel how hard it is."
Hùng: "Then sprinkle a little water on the sand and pull again." Bối (happily): "Will it feel easier or harder? I'm going to find out!" Beo (joyfully): "Me too! I pull!" Huy (happily): "Tell us what you find. Testing ideas is exactly what scientists do."
Huy: "So, how was it built? Thousands of paid workers cut stones with copper tools and sand, moved them by boat and sledge, and got them up with ramps and levers, over about 20 years."
Huy: "Here's a fun fact. The Great Pyramid was the tallest building in the world for over 3,800 years!" Bối (surprised): "Nobody built anything taller for 3,800 years? Wow!"
