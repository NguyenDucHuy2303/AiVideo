Why Is the Sky Blue? | Ask Huy
Mai (surprised): "Huy, look up! Why is the sky blue? Did somebody paint it?" Huy (laughing): "Nobody painted it, Mai. And here is a surprise. The air is not blue, and sunlight is not blue either." Mai (surprised): "Then where does all that blue come from?"
Huy (happily): "Great question! Today we will find out what sunlight is made of, how it bounces around in the air, and why the sky turns orange at sunset."
Mai (proudly): "I know! The sky is like a giant mirror. It shows us the blue sea." Huy (happily): "That is a smart guess, Mai. The sea and the sky really do look alike. Astronauts even say the whole Earth looks blue from space."
Huy: "But look at a desert, far away from any sea. The sky there is blue too. So the sea cannot be the whole story." Mai (worried): "Hmm. Then maybe air is blue, like blue juice?" Huy (happily): "Good thinking again! But a glass full of air looks clear, not blue. The real secret is hiding inside sunlight."
Huy: "Step one. Sunlight looks white, or a little yellow. But it is really a mix of many colours all travelling together."
Huy: "Shine sunlight through a special piece of glass called a prism, and it spreads out into a rainbow. Red, orange, yellow, green, blue and violet." Mai (surprised): "The rainbow was hiding inside the light all along?"
Huy (happily): "Yes! A rainbow after the rain is the same trick. Raindrops spread sunlight out into its colours."
Huy: "So here is step one. Sunlight is all the colours of the rainbow mixed together."
Huy: "Step two. Light travels in waves, a bit like the ripples on a pond, up and down, up and down."
Huy: "The distance from the top of one wave to the top of the next is called the wavelength. It just means how long each wave is."
Huy: "Every colour has its own wavelength. Red light has long, lazy waves. Blue light has short, choppy waves. Only violet has shorter ones." Mai (laughing): "Like a jump rope! Big slow swings for red, tiny fast wiggles for blue!"
Huy: "And these waves are tiny. A blue wave is about 470 nanometres long. A nanometre is one billionth of a metre. Lots of blue waves could fit side by side across one hair."
Huy: "Step two in one line. Blue light has short waves, and red light has long waves."
Huy: "Step three. Air looks empty, but it is full of tiny bits called molecules. A molecule is a tiny piece of stuff, much too small to see. Most air molecules are nitrogen and oxygen."
Huy: "When sunlight hits these molecules, some of the light bounces off in all directions. Scientists call this scattering. Scattering means spreading out every which way."
Huy: "Now here is the key. Short blue waves get scattered much, much more than long red waves. Red light mostly zooms straight on. Blue light gets bounced all over the sky."
Mai (surprised): "So blue light is like a pinball, bouncing around up there?" Huy (happily): "Exactly! It gets bounced around by molecules all across the sky."
Huy: "So wherever you look, away from the Sun, blue light is coming to your eyes from that part of the sky. That is why the whole sky glows blue."
Mai: "But why is it pale blue, not dark blue like my crayon?" Huy: "Other colours get scattered too, just much less. They mix in with the blue and make it softer and paler, a bit like blue paint with white added."
Huy: "Step three. Air scatters blue light much more than red, so blue reaches us from all over the sky."
Mai (surprised): "Then why is the sky orange and red at sunset? Did the blue run away?"
Huy: "Step four. At midday, the Sun is high, so its light takes a short trip through the air. At sunset, the Sun is low, so its light travels sideways through much more air."
Huy: "On that long trip, most of the blue gets scattered away before it reaches you. What is left is mostly orange and red. So the Sun and the clouds glow orange and red."
Mai: "And clouds? Why are they white, not blue?" Huy: "Cloud droplets are much bigger than light waves. They scatter all the colours about the same, and all colours mixed together look white. Very thick storm clouds let little light through, so they look grey."
Huy: "And at night, there is no sunlight shining on our sky to scatter, so it turns very dark blue, then black, and the stars shine through."
Huy: "Step four. At sunset, light passes through more air, so the blue is scattered away and orange and red are left."
Huy: "Now imagine standing on Mars in a space suit. The air on Mars is different from ours, so its sky has a different colour. It looks a dusty orange pink!" Mai (joyfully): "An orange pink sky! I would paint every picture that colour!"
Huy: "Here is a check you can try. This evening, with a grown-up, watch the sky while the Sun goes down. Every few minutes, draw the colours you see. Never look straight at the Sun, because it can hurt your eyes."
Mai (proudly): "Mine went from blue, to yellow, to orange, to red, and then dark blue!" Huy (happily): "You watched, you wrote it down, and you found a pattern. That is exactly how scientists work."
Huy (firmly): "So why is the sky blue? Sunlight holds every colour. Tiny molecules in the air scatter short waves like blue much more than long waves like red, so blue light comes at us from all over the sky."
Huy: "Fun fact. A scientist called Lord Rayleigh explained this back in 1871, more than 150 years ago. That is why it is called Rayleigh scattering." Mai (laughing): "Next time I see a blue sky, I will say thank you, Lord Rayleigh!"
Huy (happily): "Next time, another big question. Why is the sea salty?" Mai (laughing): "Maybe a giant spilled the salt!" Huy (happily): "Let us find out together. Subscribe so you do not miss it. Bye for now!"
