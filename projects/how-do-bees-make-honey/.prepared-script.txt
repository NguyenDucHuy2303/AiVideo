How Do Bees Make Honey?
Bối (surprised): "Huy, this honey came from bees. But bees are so tiny! How do they make something this sweet and sticky?" Huy (happily): "Great question, Bối. And here is a surprise. To make honey, bees blow bubbles. Lots and lots of tiny bubbles." Beo (laughing): "Bees blowing bubbles? Like me in the bath!"
Huy: "Today we will find out where honey really starts, what happens inside a bee, and how bees turn a runny drink into thick, golden honey. Let's fly!"
Bối (proudly): "I know! Flowers have honey inside, and bees just scoop it up and carry it home." Huy (happily): "That is a really good guess, and you are partly right. Bees do start with flowers. But flowers do not hold honey. They hold nectar. Nectar is a sweet, watery juice, much runnier than honey."
Hùng (proudly): "Look. Nectar is runny, like this water. Honey is thick, like this. Totally different!" Huy: "So something must happen to the nectar to turn it into honey. That is the part we need to find out."
Beo (worried): "Wait. Is honey bee poop?" Huy (laughing): "Lots of kids wonder that! No, it is not. Bees have a special pouch just for carrying nectar. It is separate from the stomach that digests the bee's own food. Let's see how it all works."
Huy: "Step one. It all starts with a forager bee. A forager is a bee whose job is to go out and find food. It flies to nearby flowers, usually no more than about 3 kilometres from home."
Huy: "The bee has a long tongue called a proboscis. Say it with me. Pro-BOSS-is. It works like a drinking straw, and the bee uses it to suck up the nectar." Bối (joyfully): "A built-in straw! I want one for my juice."
Huy: "The nectar goes into the bee's honey stomach, also called the honey crop. It sits just above the bee's food stomach. Think of it as a lunch box inside the bee, used to carry nectar home." Beo (happily): "My lunch box has a sandwich in it."
Huy: "The honey stomach is small. To fill it, a bee may visit more than a thousand flowers. That can take more than an hour of nonstop work." Bối (surprised): "A thousand flowers for one tummy full? Wow!"
Huy: "When it is full, the nectar weighs about half as much as the bee itself. Imagine flying home with a backpack half as heavy as you!" Hùng (tired): "Whoa, whoa! I can't even walk with this, never mind fly!" Huy: "So, step one. A bee sips watery nectar and carries it home in its honey stomach."
Huy: "Step two. Something clever happens inside the bee. The bee adds enzymes to the nectar. Enzymes are tiny helpers made by the body. These come from glands near the bee's mouth, a bit like special spit."
Huy: "The enzymes work like tiny scissors. They cut big sugars into smaller, simpler sugars. This is the start of turning nectar into honey." Hùng (proudly): "One big sugar, snip, snip, lots of little sugars!"
Huy: "Back at the hive, the forager passes the nectar to a hive bee, mouth to mouth. A hive bee is a bee whose job is to work inside the hive." Bối (laughing): "Mouth to mouth? That is like sharing a smoothie the weird way!" Beo (surprised): "Eww! But cool." Huy (happily): "A bit weird for us, but perfect for bees. And the hive bee adds even more enzymes."
Huy: "Step two takeaway. Inside the bees, enzymes start changing nectar into something new."
Huy: "Step three. Remember the bubbles? Here they are. The hive bee pushes out a little drop of nectar and holds it between its jaws, making a tiny bubble. Then it sucks it back in. Again and again." Bối (surprised): "The bees really do blow bubbles!"
Huy: "Why bubbles? A bubble spreads the nectar out thin, so lots of it touches the warm air. Then water escapes into the air faster. It is like a puddle, which dries faster when it is spread out flat."
Huy (happily): "Bees work in teams, like a relay race. One bee bubbles the nectar, then passes it on to the next. This can go on for up to 20 minutes." Beo (joyfully): "Like my race at school!"
Huy: "Then the bees put the new honey into the honeycomb. Honeycomb is made of beeswax, a wax that bees make. It has hundreds or thousands of little rooms called cells. Each cell has six sides. That shape is called a hexagon." Hùng (happily): "Count them with me. One, two, three, four, five, six sides!"
Huy: "But this honey is still too watery. So the bees keep the honey area warm, about 35 degrees Celsius, as warm as a hot summer day. And they beat their wings together, moving air over the honey to carry the water away." Bối (joyfully): "Bee fans! Whoosh!" Hùng (surprised): "Whoosh! Oops, I fanned my own hair up."
Huy: "Nectar starts out about three quarters water. Finished honey is only about one fifth water. That is why honey is so thick." Huy: "Step three takeaway. Bees dry the nectar with bubbles, warmth and fanning until it becomes thick honey."
Huy: "Step four. When the honey is ready, the bees seal each cell with a lid of wax. The lid keeps dirt out and stops the honey from drying any more."
Huy (firmly): "Honey has so much sugar and so little water that most germs cannot grow in it. Germs are tiny living things, too small to see. So honey that is stored well does not go bad." Beo (surprised): "The germs can't come in?" Huy (happily): "Some germs may get in, but most of them can't grow in it. Honey is a tough little fortress."
Huy (firmly): "Scientists think honey can last for thousands of years. They say some very old honey found by archaeologists was still safe to eat, but not everyone agrees about the oldest finds. Archaeologists are people who dig up old things to learn about the past. Honey must also be kept in a closed jar. If it soaks up water from damp air, it can spoil." Bối (surprised): "Thousands of years? That is older than Grandpa!"
Huy: "Why do bees work so hard? Honey is their food store. They eat it when it is cold or when food is hard to find. They also feed it to their babies, called larvae. Honey has much more energy than nectar, so it is a super snack."
Huy: "Beekeepers are people who look after bees. They take some of the extra honey. Good beekeepers make sure the bees keep enough food for winter." Huy (firmly): "And remember, never poke or touch a beehive. Leave hives to beekeepers in their special suits."
Huy: "Step four takeaway. Bees seal the honey in wax and save it as food for hard times."
Huy: "Imagine if you were a bee! To fill your honey stomach, you would visit more than a thousand flowers. Then you would fly home carrying a load half as heavy as you." Bối (tired): "I would need a really long nap after that!"
Huy: "Here is a safe test to try at home. Ask a grown-up for two paper towels and wet them both. Leave one flat and squash the other into a ball. Which one will dry first?" Hùng (laughing): "Oops, my sock got wet first!" Bối (proudly): "The flat one, because more of it touches the air. Just like the bee bubbles!" Huy (happily): "Exactly. Spreading out helps water escape."
Huy: "One more. With a grown-up, tip a spoon of water and a spoon of honey onto a tilted plate. The water races away. The honey crawls slowly, because the bees took most of the water out." Beo (laughing): "Honey is a snail!"
Huy: "So how do bees make honey? They sip watery nectar from flowers and carry it home in their honey stomachs. Enzymes start to change it. Then bees dry it with bubbles, warmth and fanning until it is thick and sweet, and seal it in wax."
Huy: "And here is a fun fact. One hive can make about 29 kilograms of honey in a year. That is about as heavy as a nine year old child!" Bối (laughing): "That is a lot of bubbles!"
Huy: "Next time we will ask, how do bees find their way back home? Subscribe to Ask Huy so you do not miss it!" Bối (joyfully): "Bye bye!" Beo (joyfully): "Buzz buzz!"
